  1. Rules belong to one account, never to the pipeline. Every client wants something different out of the same inbound feed, and the alternative — special-casing the processor per client — is how an ingestion codebase becomes unmaintainable. This screen is the escape hatch that keeps that logic out of the pipeline and in the hands of the person who wants it.
  2. Order is meaningful, so the interface has to say so rather than leave precedence implied by creation date. The rules are numbered, dragged into position, and the strip states the one thing that bites: a later rule overwrites an earlier one writing the same field.
  3. A rule reads as a sentence — when this, then that — instead of as a query builder. The people writing these are operators, not engineers, and a rule they cannot read at a glance is a rule they will not trust enough to switch on.
  4. A rule can be switched off without being deleted, and stays in the list greyed rather than moving to an archive. “Turn this one off and see whether the complaints stop” is the actual debugging move, and it only works if the rule keeps its position in the order.
  5. The builder names the rule’s position and what ran before it, because a rule is never evaluated alone. Conditions and results are two columns rather than one long form so the join between them stays visible — the mistake we saw most often was an operator editing a result without rereading the condition that triggers it.
  6. Preview runs against real listings and reports the blast radius before anything is saved, including conflicts with earlier rules. A rule that silently rewrites a thousand records is the exact failure this screen exists to prevent, which is also why the save queues a reprocess rather than doing it inline.
