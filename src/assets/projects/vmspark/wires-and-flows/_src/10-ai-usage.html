  1. Usage is a finance surface before it is a dashboard: it answers what we spent and on whom, and both answers get argued about later against an OpenAI invoice. So the month is a first-class control and the export is the point of the page, not a courtesy — a number you cannot take into a spreadsheet cannot be reconciled.
  2. Cache hit rate sits beside the cost it suppresses rather than in an engineering view. It is the only lever on this page anyone can actually pull, and separating the lever from the number it moves is how a cost dashboard becomes something people read once a quarter.
  3. The original of this screen had a solid dark-navy header when every other table in the app used light grey — one view, built at a different moment by a different hand, with no shared convention to fall back on. The audit found six. The fix was not restyling this table; it was that header treatment, density, striping and numeric alignment now come from one table component, so no screen gets to decide them again.
  4. Numeric columns are right-aligned with tabular figures. That is a convention rather than a preference: costs are read down the column, not across the row, and ragged decimal points make the comparison the page exists for impossible to do by eye.
  5. A totals row that reconciles against the summary cards above it. Two independently derived figures agreeing is the cheapest trust signal a finance table has, and it is also how a rollup bug gets caught by whoever reads the page rather than by nobody.
  6. Cost is attributed per account because this is a multi-tenant pipeline and AI spend bills through to clients. An aggregate figure is unactionable — knowing March cost $312 tells you nothing; knowing one client generated a quarter of it tells you where to look. Bars stay neutral: magnitude is not a state, and colour here is reserved for the ones that are.
