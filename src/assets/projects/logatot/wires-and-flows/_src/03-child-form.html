  1. Tabs rather than one long scroll. A complete record spans five unrelated concerns, and a provider enrolling a child today does not have the billing terms to hand — she has the parent in front of her. Tabs let her finish enrolment now and come back for accounting next week without scrolling past forty fields she has already answered.
  2. Toddler names are short, similar and frequently non-English, and recognition accuracy across a roster of twelve of them is the ceiling on the whole product. So name capture is treated as a first-class concern rather than a display detail: what the parser hears is matched against this list, not against the spelling in the field above it.
  3. Two dates decide the status, and a validation callback derives it — there is no Active toggle anywhere in the product. A toggle is a second source of truth, and the commonest way to get a wrong headcount is a child still marked Active six months after they left.
  4. Caregivers are created inline. Enrolment usually happens with the parent standing in the doorway, so navigating away to a separate caregiver form loses the half-typed child. The sub-form nests and saves with the parent record instead.
  5. The schedule is seven booleans rather than free text because the roster reads the same seven columns back, and health lives apart from billing because it is the tab read under pressure. Splitting the model this way costs a join on almost every screen; it buys a form nobody has to scroll to answer one question.
  6. Saving a new child queues a background job that asks a language model for the variations, pronunciations and common speech-to-text misreadings of the name, and writes them to the alias field. Corrections made in the repair queue append to the same field, so the vocabulary improves from both ends — the model guesses on day one, the provider's own mishearings refine it after that.
