Entry Step System step Branch System message Exit Design note
  1. Real menus rotate on a two-to-four week cycle, so the quickest way to plan a compliant week is to copy one that already was. The duplicate carries its validation with it — those meals were scored when they were first planned — so she starts green and spends her time on the one dish she wants to swap. It is the single biggest time saver in the feature, and it is barely a feature.
  2. Requirements are modelled as an actual matrix — age group × meal type × ingredient type × minimum amount × unit — rather than approximated with a checklist. That is the only reason the check can run here, at plan time. An approximation would still have to be reconciled against the real rule eventually, which puts the truth back at claim time and defeats the point.
  3. The flag names the gap. “Missing: 2nd fruit/veg” is one addition; “invalid meal” is a research task, and providers are not nutritionists. The short meal also stays on the calendar rather than being refused — planning happens in one sitting, so the week tolerates being incomplete and the gaps get cleared at the end.
  4. The shopping list is a by-product of a plan that already exists, which is what makes planning worth doing in a month she was not going to claim at all. Utility this week buys the data needed at month end — the feature earns its own adoption instead of depending on the discipline of someone already behind.
  5. A served meal rides the same pipeline as a nap or a diaper change, including by voice. There is no food-only habit to remember, which matters because the CACFP habit has to survive a bad week — and a habit that only pays off at month end is the first one to lapse.
  6. Month end joins two tables that already exist: the planned week and the events logged as meals were served. Nothing is transcribed, so the claim becomes a report she reviews rather than a month of lunches reconstructed from memory. Compliance falls out of the record instead of being separate work.
