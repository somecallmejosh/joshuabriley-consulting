  1. Every cell is validated against the component requirements for the age group as the menu is built, not when the claim is filed. Catching a gap here costs thirty seconds; catching it at claim time costs the meal — the food has been eaten and the money spent, and there is nothing left to fix.
  2. Duplicate week is the single biggest time saver in the feature. Real menus rotate on a two-to-four week cycle, so copying last week forward and swapping two dishes turns an hour of planning into a few minutes — which is the difference between a planner that gets used and one that does not.
  3. Requirements are modelled as a real matrix — age group by meal type by ingredient type by minimum amount and unit — rather than approximated as a rule of thumb. That is what makes plan-time validation possible at all: an approximation could only ever warn, where a matrix can say that lunch wants the fruit/veg row twice and this meal has it once.
  4. The flag names the gap. “Missing: 2nd fruit/veg,” not “invalid meal” — providers are not nutritionists, and the interface has to say which component is short so the fix is one addition rather than an evening of research.
  5. A validated meal becomes a reusable object with its component count attached, so the rules are learned once per dish instead of re-derived every week. The count travels with the meal, which is why dropping a saved one onto a day is safe by default.
  6. Serving a meal rides the same event pipeline as a nap or a diaper change, so there is no food-only workflow to remember — one habit covers every kind of record. Month end then joins two tables that already exist, which makes the claim a reconciliation, and the shopping list a free by-product of a plan she was writing anyway.
