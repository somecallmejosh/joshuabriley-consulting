Authorized pickup. A grandparent or neighbour holds their own PIN and sees only the children they are cleared for — never the whole roster.
No navigation, no back control, no account switcher — a single full-bleed card. The only exits are finishing, timing out, or handing the tablet back.
  1. Handing the tablet over signs the provider out and clears the session, keeping only the account id. A “kiosk mode” layered inside a live session is one stray back-gesture away from a parent reading another family's notes; teardown means there is no session left to escape into. The surviving account id is what lets her sign back in with one tap rather than a full login.
  2. A PIN, not a password. The parent is in a doorway with a car seat on one arm — four digits is the most authentication this moment can carry, and anything asking for an email field gets abandoned and handed back.
  3. There is no submit button. The list appears as the fourth digit lands, so the parent never has to find a second target while holding a child. A wrong PIN simply fails to resolve to anyone, which is also why it leaks nothing about which PINs exist.
  4. The list is filtered to that PIN's children before it renders, not scrolled past other families. A neighbour collecting one child sees one child, so the screen itself never discloses who else attends.
  5. The signature is drawn here, at the moment of handoff, and attached to this single attendance event. Most states require a signed record of who released a child and when — a stack of signatures collected at close of day proves nothing about any particular release.
  6. Authorized pickups are a first-class record with their own credential and their own permitted children. Custody rules belong in a data model that can be audited, not in the provider remembering who may take whom.
